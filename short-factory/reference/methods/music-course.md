# Music course for our creator-breakdown videos

Made 7 November 2026 for the Dr Plants breakdown (Paddy Galloway style: white page, narration, his podcast clips, his real footage). Why we needed it: in our first music pass the "lo-fi boom-bap" hook bed at 0:24 came out sexy and sultry, and other beds didn't fit the scene they sat under. This course puts together what ten well-known editors teach and what we measured in Paddy's own videos (Paddy sound study, sections 4, 6, 7, 9 and 10), then turns it into new prompts for our 8 house-sound cues.

How the sources were read: 7 videos through their full YouTube captions; 3 more only through their chapter list and description, because YouTube blocked further caption downloads for the day. Lessons from those 3 are marked "(from chapters/description)" and kept to what the chapters actually say. No video or audio was downloaded and nothing was paid for.

## Part 1. The sources and what each one teaches

**1. "PRO Editor Reveals How to Pick Perfect Music for ANY Video" (Austen Menges, 20-year broadcast TV commercial editor) - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=XEhoNEAYyKM - 67k views, Oct 2025**
- Before you search, spend 5 minutes writing down the one feeling you want the viewer to have. That one line saves hours and gives a better fit.
- Then pick the style for that feeling. He plays the same scene over hard rock, solo piano, folk and orchestral: each one tells a different story. Solo piano reads as romance or quiet reflection, folk reads as light travel, orchestral as drama.
- Then pick the instruments inside the style. Every instrument carries its own feeling (French horn = heroic, solo piano = intimate).
- Then the tempo: about 60 beats a minute is slow, 100 is medium, 120+ is fast. A slow track under a fast edit "just feels off".
- Then the number of instruments: fewer = calmer and more personal, more = bigger and more energetic. If one instrument spoils the mood, take it out.

**2. "How A PRO Video EDITOR Uses MUSIC" (Austen Menges) - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=-B13nDMxC1k - 26k views, Jul 2024**
- Treat picture and music as two separate layers. Beginners start and stop them together; pros change the picture while the music carries on, or change the music while the picture carries on.
- "Look" at the music: in the sound wave you can see a track's intro, its ending, and its "shifts" (quiet to loud or loud to quiet).
- Edit 1: start a track's ending on the first shot of the new scene. It gently carries the viewer from one place to the next.
- Edit 2: put a shift in the music exactly on the cut. Same track, small change of feeling.
- Edit 3 (the punchy one): no music in the first scene, then a strong intro whose first beat lands on the first frame of the next scene.

**3. "How video editors cut MUSIC for perfect timing" (Standard Story Company) - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=ZbVmyH9NVnk - 181k views, Oct 2022**
- Start the music about 12 dB lower than you think, then adjust.
- A beat drop must never land in the middle of a sentence. Put it where the speaker stops and the pictures take over.
- Loop: copy one bar of the track to make it longer, with a tiny crossfade so there is no click at the join.
- "Sound up": drop the music out completely so a line can land on its own, then bring the music back just in time to hit the next beat.
- "Ring out": end a track on one big note with lots of echo, instead of a sudden stop.
- Do the story edit first; then make the music fit the story. Never let the music dictate the edit.

**4. "How to choose the RIGHT music for your video" (Matti Haapoja) - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MF3tJv0qL9Q - 159k views, Dec 2022**
- The biggest tip: forget your personal taste and forget genres. Search by mood (epic, dreamy, dark, happy, eccentric), not by style.
- One track per part of the story. Changing the track tells the viewer "something new is starting".
- No vocals under a voiceover; the two fight each other.
- Every genre sends a signal. If the music says "anger" (his example: heavy metal) but your story says "happy", the music is wrong no matter how good it is.
- Some tracks are meant to be quiet background and must not draw attention; others drive the edit. Know which job each track has.
- He took two tracks from the same artist so the video still feels like one piece.

**5. "How To Edit A Documentary Like Johnny Harris" (Motion Array Tutorials) - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=nZrPVUzYHKE - 541k views, Jan 2022**
- Johnny's style feels "real and touchable": paper, wood, old photos, film grain, and sound to match (an old projector tick as a still photo moves into place).
- When a word flashes across many pages, add small, quiet ticks on each change.
- The feeling to aim for: someone is giving you a presentation at a desk, not a TV advert.
- Don't copy a creator wholesale. Take one or two pieces you like and make them your own.

**6. "3 Sound Design Secrets That Feel Like CHEATING" (Herman Huang) - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=tzfzkTAQsnE - 1.08M views, Apr 2025**
- Instead of just turning the music down, cut a small "pocket" out of the music's middle range (where the voice lives). The voice then sits in that gap and the music can stay fuller.
- Commit to one family of sounds per video. Gritty bass hits don't belong in a corporate film, clean bell dings don't belong in a streetwear ad. Random sounds from everywhere make a video feel cheap.
- "Emotional realism": he replaced coffee-making sounds with nature sounds (grounds breaking = ice cracking, a swish = an ocean wave). Sounds can carry the meaning, not just copy the picture. This suits a nature channel.
- To ease into a song: reverse its first beat and use it as a short swell leading in.
- To leave a song: cut before the next beat and put a big echo on the last one.
- Layer a soft swell under the way in and a hit under the way out for a seamless change.

**7. "The ONLY 3 Sound Effects You Need to Tell Any Story" (Tim Runia) - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=JOm_EqBoW0M - 317k views, Mar 2026**
- He used to collect thousands of sounds; in the end the same few did all the work.
- Whooshes = movement. Heavy and slow feels serious; light and quick feels fun or comic.
- Risers (swells) = tension, "something is coming". They hit hardest when they cut straight into silence.
- Impacts = "this moment matters". His favourite: the music cuts out and a deep hit lands as the new shot appears.
- Good editing is tension and release: the swell is the tension, the arrival is the release.
- Clicks (shutter, pen, mouse) make quick sequences feel snappy.

**8. "How Johnny Harris uses Music to Tell Stories" (Mandelbro, a composer) - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=K8Vo1a4YtBo - 32k views, Aug 2022 (from chapters/description)**
- Johnny's videos have an opening theme: a recurring musical signature the audience learns to recognise.
- In some places the music drives the edit: the cuts follow the music.
- In other places the story drives the music: the music changes because the story turns.
- His references credit producer Tom Fox (tfbeats) for Johnny's music: one producer's sound runs through the channel, which is part of why it's recognisable.

**9. "How to Always Choose the Right Music for Any Video" (The Bhavya Shah) - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=KtSh7PGqmBE - 109k views, Feb 2026, with Part 1 "How to Use Music for 10X Storytelling" https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OwvYtQqgQuc - 98k views (from chapters/description)**
- His "REC triangle" for choosing a track: Rhythm (does its pace match the cuts?), Emotion (does it feel right?), Context (does it fit the place, the subject and the moment?).
- Choose tracks with natural cut points and clear beats; they are easy to edit.
- Think like a director: study how film composers use particular instruments to trigger particular feelings, then choose music on purpose.
- A long piece can be cut down hard (he cuts a 7-minute score to 40 seconds) as long as the cuts land on the music's own phrases.

**10. "Proper Audio Levels for Video Editors (Dialogue, Music & SFX)" (Jason Yadlovski) - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=sbCd91VG22w - 38.5k views, Jan 2026 (from chapters/description)**
- There is an order of importance: voice first, then music, then effects.
- Music should "support, don't compete" with the voice.
- Decide on purpose whether each effect should stand out or blend in.
- A loudness setting at the end (LUFS, the overall loudness number) will not fix a bad mix. Get the balance right first.
- Use the same simple routine on every video so you stop guessing.

Also checked: **"Paddy Galloway Tells Us How To Edit YouTube For TV" (The Editing Podcast) - https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=t2M0C2tuHK8 - 63k views, Nov 2023.** Paddy hardly talks about music here, but one point matters: more and more people watch YouTube on a TV, so editing is moving away from nervous, gimmick-heavy cutting ("insecure editing", the host's phrase) towards calmer, TV-documentary pacing that leaves room for the story. Calm, confident music fits that.

## Part 2. The course (12 lessons)

**Lesson 1. Write down the feeling before you write the prompt.**
Rule: one line per part of the video: what should the viewer feel here? ("curious", "this guy is clever", "uh-oh", "now I get it").
Why: Menges and Matti both say this is the step that saves hours and gives the best fit. Our first pass started from Paddy's style names ("lo-fi boom-bap"), not from a feeling, and the AI filled the gap with its own idea of the style.
How we apply it: every cue prompt below starts with the feeling and the scene it sits under, before any style words.

**Lesson 2. Describe the mood and the instruments, and be careful with genre words.**
Rule: lead with mood and instruments; use genre names only as a light hint, and always say which neighbouring genres you don't want.
Why: every genre carries baggage (Matti: heavy metal says anger even if your story is happy). To an AI music model, "slow lo-fi with soft Rhodes, muted guitar, deep sub bass, confident" sits right next to late-night R&B and neo-soul, which is why our 0:24 cue came out sultry. Menges: the instruments carry the feeling (solo piano = romance or reflection).
How we apply it: name concrete instruments that read as "curious, clever, outdoors" (marimba, kalimba, plucked strings, acoustic guitar picking, glockenspiel, clean piano, light hand percussion) and add an "Avoid" list to every prompt.

**Lesson 3. One bed per part; changing the music means "new chapter".**
Rule: switch the music at each change of part (step card, after a clip, at the turn), and only there.
Why: Matti: a new track tells the viewer something new is starting. Paddy does exactly this: one bed per part, switched at the step card or straight after a creator clip.
How we apply it: the placement map in Part 3. No music change in the middle of a step.

**Lesson 4. Music supports the voice; it never competes with it.**
Rule: no vocals or vocal sounds, no busy lead tune under speech, set the level once per part and leave it.
Why: Yadlovski: voice first, music "support, don't compete". Matti: no vocals under voiceover. Paddy keeps his normal beds 18-27 dB under his voice, the hook beat 8-11 dB under, the "thinking" pad 25-33 dB under, and never pumps the music up and down (no ducking: it's no louder in his pauses).
How we apply it: ask the AI for music "made to sit under a narrator", with the tune in the gaps, not on top. In the edit, set one level per bed; Herman Huang's trick of a fixed small dip in the music's middle range (where the voice sits) lets us keep beds clear without pumping.

**Lesson 5. Silence is the loudest sound we have.**
Rule: cut all music for 0.5-1.5 s just before a punch word or big number, and 10-15 s of almost no music once or twice for the key idea. Bring it back on the next sentence or the next beat.
Why: Paddy does this 9-11 times a video; it's his main "effect". Standard Story's "sound up" is the same move. Tim Runia: a swell hits hardest when it cuts into silence.
How we apply it: plan the silences in the script ("He doesn't make it", the growth number, the named last step). We don't need the AI to make the silences; we cut them in the edit.

**Lesson 6. Cut the music to the story, not the story to the music.**
Rule: lock the voice and pictures first, then move, loop and trim the music so its strong moments land in the gaps between lines.
Why: Standard Story: story first; a beat drop in the middle of a sentence is "no good"; loop a bar to make a track longer. Bhavya Shah: pick tracks with natural cut points so this is easy.
How we apply it: ask for loopable beds with clear four-bar phrases and no big drops or solos in the middle (we add our own drops with silence). Seams get a tiny crossfade.

**Lesson 7. Enter and leave a track on purpose.**
Rule: each bed starts in one of three ways and ends in one of three ways.
- Start: (a) hard start, with the first beat on the first frame of the new part (Menges's punchy edit; ours for the hook and the last step); (b) fade in under the first sentence of a calm part; (c) a short reversed swell leading in (Huang).
- End: (a) dead stop into silence before a punch line (Paddy); (b) ring-out with a big echo on the last beat (Standard Story, Huang); (c) let one bed's ending carry over the first shot of the next part (Menges).
Why: sudden, unplanned starts and stops are what make music feel "random" or "unrelated to the scene".
How we apply it: every cue is asked for with a clean start and a clean final hit, so we have something to cut on.

**Lesson 8. Build tension before the payoff, then release it.**
Rule: one build in the video, right before the last step, landing on silence or the return of the hook beat.
Why: Tim Runia: swell = tension, arrival = release. Paddy uses a 20-second drumless swell into his last step (Squid Game) and brings the hook beat back for it.
How we apply it: cue M6a (the build) runs into a dead stop; the named last step starts on the hook bed's first beat.

**Lesson 9. Few sound effects, all from one family.**
Rule: about one clearly audible effect a minute, only at changes of part. All effects from one "natural, handmade" family (wood, paper, soil, leaves, water).
Why: Paddy uses almost none (labels, circles and camera moves are silent). Huang: commit to one sound style; mixing random packs feels cheap. Runia: a few sounds used well beat thousands. Huang's "emotional realism" (nature sounds standing in for other actions) fits a nature channel.
How we apply it: the 22 effects already planned in the Paddy sound study are all from the garden family. Keep to them.

**Lesson 10. The creator's clips carry their own sound.**
Rule: music fully out 0.5-1 s before a Dr Plants clip; the clip plays with its own sound at about voice level; our music comes back on our first word after it, usually as a different bed.
Why: it's what Paddy does every time, and it stops our music clashing with the creator's own music.
How we apply it: never lay a bed under his clips. His footage brings its own music and nature sounds.

**Lesson 11. Build a house sound: 5-8 recurring tracks and one signature.**
Rule: the same small set of beds in every breakdown, each with a fixed job.
Why: Paddy reuses about 5 tracks across videos. Johnny Harris has an opening theme and a credited producer (Mandelbro). Matti used one artist so the film felt like one piece.
How we apply it: our 8 cues are made once, carefully, and become the channel's house sound. Every one of them must pass the checklist in Part 3 before it's used, because we'll hear it in every video.

**Lesson 12. Judge the music with the voice off, then on a phone.**
Rule: listen to each bed alone first (does the music on its own say the right thing?), then under the voice at phone-speaker volume.
Why: a sultry or cheesy bed hides under narration on studio headphones and jumps out on its own. Yadlovski: the end-of-mix loudness setting won't fix a wrong balance.
How we apply it: the checklist at the end of Part 3.

## Part 3. Fixing our music

### What went wrong at 0:24, and why
The hook bed asked for "lo-fi boom-bap at 78 bpm, dusty drums, deep sub bass, muted guitar, soft Rhodes chords, confident, head-nod". Each word is reasonable alone, but together, at a slow swung tempo, they describe the late-night R&B / neo-soul / "bedroom" sound: lazy swung drums, round sub bass, jazzy electric-piano chords and muted guitar. That's why it sounded sexy. Paddy's real hook beat works because of what's around it (bright story, fast narration, white page); an AI model asked for those words alone drifts towards the sensual version.

The other "unrelated" beds had the same problem in milder form: prompts named a style ("chill electronic", "lo-fi flute groove") but not the subject or the job, so the model made generic café or lounge music with nothing to do with plants, animals, survival or strategy.

### Words that pull music towards sexy, sleazy, cheesy or off-topic: don't use these
- **Sexy or sultry:** saxophone, smooth jazz, R&B, neo-soul, slow jam, Rhodes or "soft electric piano" at slow tempos, muted or wah guitar, deep round sub bass at under 85 bpm, "smooth", "silky", "velvet", "sensual", "late night", "lush", "seductive", breathy vocals, vocal chops, moans, "ooh"s, jazzy 7th and 9th chords.
- **Lounge or hotel lobby:** bossa nova, lounge, chillhop café, vinyl crackle and "mellow" together, trip-hop, downtempo.
- **Cheesy:** ukulele and whistling, hand claps with glockenspiel ("corporate happy"), stadium "triumphant", inspirational piano with big string swells, kazoo, cartoon tuba or bassoon, game-show sounds.
- **Off-topic drama:** epic trailer drums, dark horror drones, "tragic" cinematic strings, dubstep drops, EDM build-ups.
- **Any vocals at all**, including wordless "aah" choirs.

### Words to use instead
- **Curiosity (questions, mysteries):** pizzicato strings (plucked violins), marimba, kalimba, glockenspiel, celesta, soft felt piano, light woodblock, "inquisitive", "investigative", "documentary underscore", "sparse", "playful but intelligent".
- **Nature (his world: desert, ants, jungle, frogs):** acoustic guitar fingerpicking, kalimba, wooden flute only if bright and playful (not breathy), hand percussion (shaker, frame drum), marimba, "nature documentary", "organic", "earthy", "outdoors", "morning".
- **Business explainer (the strategy, numbers, steps):** clean plucked synth, light piano or mallet patterns repeating like a clock, crisp tight snare or rimshot, steady straight (not swung) beat at 90-110 bpm, "smart", "focused", "explainer", "tech-documentary", "confident and clear".
- **Shape words that help every cue:** "instrumental", "background music made to sit under a narrator", "melody stays simple and leaves room for speech", "loopable with clear 4-bar phrases", "clean start, clean final hit", "simple bright chords", "polished production".

### The 8 cues, rewritten for ElevenLabs Music
Each prompt is written as plain text for the board's music boxes. Where the box allows a separate "avoid" or negative-styles field (ElevenLabs Music's composition plan has one per section), put the Avoid list there; otherwise keep the "Avoid:" sentence at the end of the prompt. ElevenLabs' own guide says the first section's styles set the tone for the whole track, so the most important words come first. Tempo and key can be written in as styles (for example "92 BPM", "D major").

**M1 Seedling intro (15-20 s): cold open, the big number**
Prompt: "Instrumental background music for the cold open of a curious nature-and-strategy documentary on YouTube, made to sit under a narrator. Feeling: 'wait, what?', light and intrigued. Bright marimba and kalimba playing a simple questioning motif, soft pizzicato strings, a little glockenspiel, light woodblock ticks, no drums, 100 BPM, D major, simple bright chords, sparse, clean start, ends on a short rising pickup that leads into a beat. Polished production."
Avoid: "vocals, saxophone, jazz chords, lounge, R&B, cartoon tuba or bassoon, ukulele, whistling, hand claps, cinematic strings, dark drones."

**M2 Greenhouse beat (90 s loop): the hook and the named last step**
Prompt: "Instrumental confident head-nod beat for the hook of a smart YouTube explainer about how a nature creator outgrew everyone, made to sit under a fast narrator. Feeling: clever, sure of itself, 'this is going to be good'. 92 BPM, straight (not swung) groove, crisp tight snare and punchy kick, clean bass that follows the kick, a short catchy plucked-strings and marimba riff, light piano stabs on simple major and minor triads, shaker, documentary hip-hop, bright and dry, loopable with clear 4-bar phrases, no big drops, clean start on the first beat, clean final hit. Polished production."
Avoid: "vocals, vocal chops, saxophone, smooth jazz, R&B, neo-soul, slow jam, Rhodes, soft electric piano, muted guitar, wah guitar, sub-bass wobble, sensual, sultry, late night, lounge, jazzy 7th and 9th chords, vinyl crackle, trap hi-hat rolls, dubstep."
(Main change: faster and straight instead of slow and swung; plucked strings, marimba and piano instead of Rhodes and muted guitar; clean bass instead of deep sub bass. This is the fix for the 0:24 problem.)

**M3 Root question pad (60 s loop): "What does he know that we don't?"**
Prompt: "Instrumental, almost silent thinking music under a narrator asking a question, for a curious nature documentary. Feeling: quiet question, a small mystery. Drumless, soft felt piano and celesta playing slow single notes, a very soft warm string pad, occasional glockenspiel twinkle, slow chord changes, 70 BPM, A minor that resolves to C major, very sparse, nothing that pulls attention, loopable."
Avoid: "vocals, choir, saxophone, Rhodes, jazz chords, R&B, lounge, horror drones, dark ambient, tragic strings, big swells, drums."

**M4 Steady growth groove (2 min loop): Step 1, the long explaining**
Prompt: "Instrumental steady explainer groove for a YouTube business-strategy breakdown, made to sit under a narrator for minutes without tiring the ear. Feeling: focused, clear, quietly moving forward. 96 BPM, straight soft kick and rimshot, light shaker, a repeating plucked-synth and marimba pattern like a ticking clock, clean round bass, simple bright chords, very little melody, loopable with clear 4-bar phrases, no drops or solos, polished modern documentary sound."
Avoid: "vocals, saxophone, smooth jazz, R&B, neo-soul, Rhodes, wobbly synths, lounge, chillhop café, vinyl crackle, corporate ukulele, whistling, hand claps, EDM build-ups."
(Main change: "warm wobbly synth chords" and "chill electronic" pulled towards lounge; now it's a clean "clock" pattern, which reads as "explaining".)

**M5 Field groove (2 min loop): Step 2, the title/cover/screenshot breakdown**
Prompt: "Instrumental light, playful groove for the busy middle of a nature-creator strategy video, under a narrator going through titles, covers and screenshots. Feeling: nimble, curious, a little mischievous, outdoors. 98 BPM, straight groove, acoustic guitar fingerpicking, kalimba melody in short phrases with gaps for speech, hand percussion (shaker, frame drum), light upright-bass plucks, bright and dry, loopable with clear 4-bar phrases."
Avoid: "vocals, breathy flute, pan flute, saxophone, smooth jazz, bossa nova, lounge, R&B, Rhodes, vinyl crackle, sensual, exotic, tribal chanting, ukulele, whistling."
(Main change: the breathy wooden flute over slow lo-fi is the second most likely sultry or "exotic lounge" sound; replaced with guitar and kalimba. If you want to keep a flute for Paddy's feel, write "bright, short, playful wooden recorder phrases", not "breathy".)

**M6a Sprout build (20 s): just before the last step**
Prompt: "Instrumental 20-second build for a YouTube documentary, right before the big reveal. Feeling: something is coming, rising curiosity. No drums. Starts almost silent with a soft string pad and slow marimba, then adds pizzicato strings and a rising glockenspiel pattern, getting brighter and louder step by step, a soft reversed swell in the last 2 seconds, ends with a hard stop into silence on the last beat. D major."
Avoid: "vocals, choir, epic trailer drums, braams, horror, dubstep or EDM riser, saxophone, R&B."

**M6b Outro groove (60 s): summary, subscribe, end card**
Prompt: "Instrumental warm, upbeat outro for a smart nature-creator documentary on YouTube, under a narrator's summary. Feeling: satisfied, 'now I get it', friendly. 100 BPM, straight groove, the same plucked-strings and marimba riff family as the main beat but brighter, light synth arpeggio, crisp snare, clean bass, simple major chords, ends on one final hit with a natural ring-out. Polished production."
Avoid: "vocals, saxophone, smooth jazz, R&B, Rhodes, triumphant stadium, corporate ukulele, whistling, hand claps, inspirational piano with big strings, EDM drop."
(Main change: "happy, triumphant, feel good" is the shortest road to cheesy advert music; now it's "satisfied, friendly" with a clean ending to cut on.)

**M7 Reflective piano (40 s): the setback ("He doesn't make it", the flop)**
Prompt: "Instrumental quiet, honest moment in a nature documentary after bad news, under a narrator. Feeling: a small, real loss, restrained, not dramatic. Solo felt piano, slow single notes and open fifths, a very soft low string note underneath, lots of space, 64 BPM, D minor, no build, fades to silence."
Avoid: "vocals, choir, tragic cinematic strings, big swells, melodrama, saxophone, jazz chords, R&B, romantic piano ballad, rain sounds."
(Main change: "sad, emotional" invites a soap-opera piano ballad, and Menges warns solo piano can read as romance. "Restrained, honest, open fifths, lots of space" keeps it serious and short. Always start this part with 1-2 s of silence first.)

### Before you press Generate (cheap habits)
- Make one version of M2 first (it's the one that went wrong) and check it against the checklist below before making the rest.
- Listen to the first 20 seconds of every version with the voice off. If it already sounds like a hotel bar, a bedroom or an advert, throw it out.
- Keep the riff family consistent: M2 and M6b should sound related (same instruments), so the hook, the last step and the outro feel like one house sound.

### Checklist to review a music pass by ear
1. **Voice off:** play each bed alone for 20 s. Does it say "curious", "clever" or "outdoors"? Or does it say bedroom, hotel lobby, advert, horror film or sad soap opera? Anything in the second group goes.
2. **The colleague test:** if someone walked in, would the music alone make them think it's a nature/strategy documentary?
3. **No voices:** no singing, "ooh"s, breaths or vocal chops anywhere.
4. **Every word clear on a phone speaker** at normal volume, and the music still just audible under speech.
5. **One bed per part:** the music changes at step cards, after clips and at the turn, and nowhere else.
6. **Silences in place:** music fully out for 0.5-1.5 s before each punch word or big number, and before every Dr Plants clip.
7. **His clips are clean:** no bed playing under his footage.
8. **No pumping:** the music doesn't swell up in every little pause.
9. **Strong moments in gaps:** beat drops, riffs and hits land between sentences, never on top of a key word.
10. **Clean ins and outs:** each bed starts on a picture change or under a first word, and ends on a dead stop, a ring-out or a carry-over. No random fades.
11. **One build, one payoff:** the swell into the last step ends in silence, then the hook beat hits on the step card.
12. **Seams:** loop joins are inaudible (no click, no jump in the groove).
13. **Effects:** about one clearly audible effect a minute, all from the garden family.
14. **The ending:** a clear final hit or ring-out on the last word, then silence.
